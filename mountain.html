Montenegro's vertical escape.

From the Adriatic coast to glacial peaks above 2,500 m — Montenegro packs more dramatic elevation change per kilometre than almost anywhere in Europe. These are the mountains worth lacing your boots for.

01

Durmitor

Durmitor is Montenegro's ultimate hiking destination — a UNESCO massif of limestone peaks, glacial cirques, and high plateaus centred above Žabljak. The summit of Bobotov Kuk (2,523 m) is a full-day return hike that rewards with views across half the Balkans. But Durmitor isn't only about the highest point: trails wind past eighteen glacial lakes, through dense black pine forest, and along the rim of the Tara Canyon far below. It's equally at home in winter boots as hiking ones.

Highest peak2,523 m
Base townŽabljak
Glacial lakes18
02

Bjelasica

Bjelasica is a long, forested ridge running through the heart of Montenegro, gentle enough for beginners yet rewarding for experienced hikers seeking solitude. Its highest point, Crna Glava (2,139 m), rises above a landscape of beech and spruce that hides Biogradsko jezero — one of Europe's last primeval forests. The mountain also forms the backbone of the Bjelasica ski resort around Kolašin, making it a year-round destination. Trails here are quiet, well-marked, and surrounded by the kind of green that city people come a long way to find.

Highest peak2,139 m
Base townKolašin
03

Komovi

Three jagged peaks — Kučki Kom (2,487 m), Vasojevićki Kom (2,461 m), and Ljevoriječki Kom (2,469 m) — make Komovi one of the most visually striking massifs in the entire Western Balkans. The approach from the village of Štavna is a serious undertaking: steep, loose, and utterly magnificent. There are no crowds here and no easy shortcuts — just raw ridge walking, exposed scrambles, and the profound satisfaction of summits that have to be earned. For experienced hikers, Komovi is the peak of a Montenegro trip in every sense.

Highest peak2,487 m
Base villageŠtavna
Peaks3
04

Prokletije & Visitor

The Accursed Mountains live up to their name — wild, remote, and staggeringly beautiful. Montenegro's highest point, Zla Kolata (2,534 m), rises from a landscape of sharp limestone ridges, hidden glacial lakes, and valleys that have barely changed in centuries. The adjacent Visitor massif adds even more untamed terrain, with its own network of trails linking remote katuns and viewpoints that few tourists ever reach. This is frontier hiking — self-sufficient, unhurried, and deeply rewarding for those prepared for it.

Highest peak2,534 m
Base townGusinje / Plav
Mammal species50+
05

Orjen

Orjen is the highest karst massif along the entire Adriatic coast — a dramatic limestone wilderness that rises sharply above the Bay of Kotor and receives more rainfall than almost anywhere else in Europe, feeding a landscape of dramatic sinkholes, snow-filled depressions, and dense oak and beech forests. The summit of Zubački Kabao (1,894 m) offers a remarkable contrast: the glittering Adriatic behind you, wild Herzegovinian plateaus ahead. Trails connect the Montenegrin and Bosnian sides, making Orjen ideal for multi-day border-crossing hikes.

Highest peak1,894 m
Base townHerceg Novi
06

Lovćen

Lovćen may be Montenegro's most accessible mountain, but it's far from its least rewarding. The trails above Cetinje wind through rocky Mediterranean scrub and ancient terraced villages, climbing to panoramas that take in the Bay of Kotor, the Adriatic, and on clear days, the mountains of Albania. The hike to Jezerski Vrh and the Njegoš mausoleum is a cultural pilgrimage as much as a physical one — 461 stone steps carved into the cliff rewarding you with one of the finest viewpoints in the country. A perfect half-day from the coast.

Highest peak1,749 m
Base townCetinje
Steps to top461
07

Sinjajevina

Sinjajevina is the largest mountain plateau in Europe — a vast, rolling highland that stretches for kilometres in every direction without a single sharp peak to interrupt the sky. This is a different kind of mountain experience: wide open, windswept, and deeply pastoral. In summer, shepherds still move their flocks across the plateau as they have for centuries, camping in stone katuns scattered across the grassland. Hiking here is more about space than summits — long traverses, enormous skies, and a quietness that is becoming rare anywhere on the continent.

Highest point2,276 m
Plateau size~500 km²
08

Hajla

Sitting on the border between Montenegro and Kosovo, Hajla (2,403 m) is one of the Balkans' best-kept hiking secrets. The ascent from Rožaje takes you through dense conifer forest before opening onto a long, airy ridge with views stretching from the Prokletije in the south to the Serbian highlands in the north. The summit itself is broad and grassy — a rare flat-topped peak in a region defined by jagged ridges. Hajla sees few foreign visitors, which means quiet trails, genuine local hospitality in the villages below, and a sense of discovery that is increasingly hard to find.

Highest peak2,403 m
Base townRožaje
